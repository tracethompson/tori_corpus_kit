Re: Comments on &quot;Draft Guidances Relating to the Regulation of Human Cells, Tissues, or Cellular or Tissue-Based Products&quot; <br/><br/>I wish to comment on the concerned draft guidance and propose introduction of certain measures that would facilitate informed decision-making by stakeholders involved. All participants in the system need reliable and accurate information to make better treatment decisions: patients who consider stem cell treatments, healthcare professionals who care for these patients before and after such treatment, and payers, who may or may not wish to include these therapies in their programs depending on their benefits, risks, and cost-effectiveness. <br/><br/>The Helsinki Declaration says on unproven Interventions in Clinical Practice:<br/>37. In the treatment of an individual patient, where proven interventions do not exist or other known interventions have been ineffective, the physician, after seeking expert advice, with informed consent from the patient or a legally authorized representative, may use an unproven intervention if in the physician&#39;s judgement it offers hope of saving life, re-establishing health or alleviating suffering. This intervention should subsequently be made the object of research, designed to evaluate its safety and efficacy. In all cases, new information must be recorded and, where appropriate, made publicly available.<br/><br/>Most stem cell interventions are not reimbursed by insurers. Most of the time, patients have to pay for these treatments themselves. Patients are making decisions based on information they can find in public domain. Currently, it is very difficult to accurately evaluate the benefit:risk profile of stem cell treatments. Because procedures under 21 CFR part 1271 (CGTP) do not qualify as studies, treatment outcomes of these interventions are never made available to the public. <br/>Interventions offered by establishments under 21 CFR part 1271 (CGTP) rely on studies that have already been done and published, as well as their own empirical experience from own medical practice. Brief overview of registered studies and published studies illustrate the scope and extent of the information gap on stem cell treatments and their outcomes. <br/><br/>Creation of a registry <br/>To facilitate informed decision-making by patients, healthcare professionals, and insurers, I am suggesting creation of a registry into which information on stem cell interventions conducted under 21 CFR part 1271 would be reported. To make such undertaking worthwhile, the anonymized dataset would have to include indication, type of treatment, and treatment outcome. Not only this would greatly benefit patients, who would be able to make truly informed choice for themselves; this registry could also benefit healthcare professionals who would be able to compare stem cell interventions to other existing therapies. <br/><br/>In total, 5,298 studies conducted with stem cells globally as of January 7, 2016 (ClinicalTrials.gov). Most studies are conducted in highly regulated markets in the U.S. and in Europe. Other popular destinations include China, Iran, Israel and Middle America. (FIG 1) <br/><br/>The register lists wide variety of conditions studied in a manner that is difficult to assess. Many conditions are listed under wide variety of terms, often overlapping, some low level, and others very general and high level. It is therefore difficult to judge whether a study intervention should be at all included in a specific evaluation without detailed scrutiny of the study objectives and design. This task is challenging even for an experienced biomedical researcher, and virtually impossible for a non-healthcare professional. <br/><br/>Stem cell therapies potentially offer great benefits to patients whose current options are either limited or non-existent. Some patients are willing to undergo new, experimental and unproven therapies, to take advantage of this cutting-edge research. These people are taking all the risks associated with novel therapies and procedures upon themselves.<br/>To protect the interests of patients, and to support innovation in this rapidly developing field of medicine, it would be wise to facilitate substantial improvement of the information environment, and to provide all parties involved with the information they need to make better treatment decisions. <br/><br/>

 The FDA has proposed the regulation of human cells, tissues, and cellular and tissue based products (HCT/Ps) to prevent the introduction, transmission, and spread of communicable diseases; however, these regulations infringe on human rights and stall innovation in the stem cell research industry. Additional time has been given from the original proposed date of the regulatory hearing because there is considerable interest in this topic and a need for stakeholder comments. Groups affected by this regulation include, but are not limited to: tissue establishments, biological and device manufacturers, health care professionals, clinicians, biomedical researchers, and the public. Comments requested from this group should be directed towards the scope, topics, questions, additional issues that need guidance, and the clarity of the regulations. The FDA has also scheduled a scientific workshop to identify and discuss the scientific considerations and the challenges of HCT/Ps in the market (regulations.gov).   HCT/Ps are articles consisting of human cells or tissues that are intended for implantation, transplantation, or transfer into a human. HCT/Ps are currently regulated under Section 361 of the Public Health Service Act, originally intended to prevent the spread of diseases from foreign countries. The Public Health Service Act also established the government’s authority to quarantine individuals with harmful communicable diseases. HCT/Ps are regulated if they meet certain criteria. If they are: minimally manipulated, intended for homologous use, 1there is no combination of cells or tissue with another article (except water, crystalloids, or a  For HCT/Ps to be intended for homologous use, they need to be repurposed for the same 1function that they originally existed.  sterilizing or preserving agent), they do not have a systemic effect and is not dependent on the 2metabolic activity of living cells, they do have systemic effect and depend on metabolic activity of living cells and are used autologous-ly, they are under regulation. If HCT/Ps don’t follow 3under these criterion, they are regulated as a drug, device, or biological product under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act. The FDA has issued four draft guidances to further explain the criteria of the HCT/Ps to be regulated including the same-surgical-procedure exception, the minimal manipulation of HCT/PS, HCT/Ps from Adipose tissue and the homologous use of 4HCT/Ps. If someone has the ability and wants to repurpose their stem cells for future rehabilitation, why shouldn’t they be allowed to?  For HCT/Ps to be systemic, they depend on the rest of the body’s functions to survive and 2function. To be non-systemic, HCT/Ps do not depend on the function of anything else to survive and function.  For HCT/Ps to be considered for autologous purposes, they can only be reused in the same 3body they were taken from. The American Thoracic Society (ATS) has been closely following the development of cell -based 
therapeutic modalities for respiratory diseases such as COPD and cystic fibrosis. As patient access to safe and effective cell and tissue- based therapies strongly depends on FDA regulatory 
requirements, we find of particular interest the new FDA draft guidances for industry. We specifically refer to “Minimal Manipulation of Human Cells, Tissues, and Cellular and Tissue -
Based Products”, “Human Cells, Tissues, and Cellular and Tissue -Based Products (HCT/Ps) 
from Adipose Tissue: Regulatory Considerations”, “Homologous Use of Human Cells, Tissues, and Cellular and Tissue -Based Products”, and “Same Surgical Procedure Exception under 21

 In general, we consider the new guidances to be helpful by clarifying key definitions such as 
“homologous use”, “minimal manipulation” and “surgical exemption” and by removing vulnerable “gray areas” in the existing FDA regulat ions that have allowed for the appearance of 
hundreds of US clinics (Knoepfler and Turner, Cell Stem Cell, 2016; Berger et al, Cell Stem Cell , 
2016) offering unregulated and unproven stem cell -based therapies. Despite the non- binding 
nature of the guidance s, they may encourage systematic regulatory actions regarding this type of 
“clinic” outfits, which will be welcomed by our professional society. We provide detailed comments for each guidance below.  
 
1) “Minimal Manipulation of Human Cells, Tissues, and Cellu lar and Tissue -Based 
Products”  
Title 21 of the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) Part 1271 defines, among others, the criteria that exempt human cells, tissues, and cellular and tissue -based products (HCT/Ps) from the 
requirements of section 351 of the PHS  Act (regulation as drug or device including pre -market 
review). One of the criteria, namely “minimal manipulation” had not been clearly defined in the past and this has been used as a “loophole” for outfits that offer unregulated cell based- therapies 
for various ailments, including lung diseases.

There are several examples of structural and non -structural tissue manipulation in this guidance 
and we believe the more relevant ones to tissue/cell products for lung diseases are Points 10 and 
12. The ATS agrees with the example given in Point 10 that considers processing of adipose tissue as more than minimal manipulation. This implies that the stromal vascular fraction (SVF) commonly isolated from dissociation of subcutaneous adipose tissue would be regulated as a drug and we concur with this view (see additional comments for the Adipose Tissue -related 
guidance below). We also agree with Point 12 that any processing - including, but not limited to, 
ex vivo expansion-  “that alters any relevant biological characteristics of cells or nonstructural 
tissues ge nerally would be considered more than minimal manipulation”.  
 Nevertheless, there are other passages in the draft guidance that are unclear and may re -introduce 
ambiguity in the interpretation of the guidance. For example, there is not a clear definition of tissues that have a dual role, i.e. perform both structural and non- structural (metabolic) functions 
such as fat tissue (Point 2 of the guidance). This may cause confusion as some manipulations may alter the structural but not the metabolic function and  vice versa. Furthermore, the sentence 
“…separation of structural tissue into components in which the relevant characteristics relating to reconstruction, repair, or replacement are not altered generally would be considered minimal manipulation…” from Point 7 needs clarification. Does the sentence refer to all components of the tissue? Would, for instance, the fat fraction and the SVF originating from enzymatic treatment of fat tissue be considered minimally manipulated?  
 
2) “Homologous Use of Human Cells, Tis sues, and Cellular and Tissue -Based 
Products”  
We welcome this guidance that is supposed to further clarify the concept of “homologous use” 
for HCT/Ps. Although the guidance provides several examples of homologous and non-homologous use for various cell/tis sue products, it does not directly address the two major 
unregulated cell therapies currently advertised and administered to patients with respiratory 
diseases, namely SVF and unexpanded mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs). We presume that the 
sentence “Gener ally, if an HCT/P is intended for use as an unproven treatment for a myriad of 
diseases or conditions, the HCT/P is likely not intended for homologous use only” in Q&A 1 indirectly refers to such products but we think additional examples will be useful. Most of the providers of SVF and unexpanded MSCs claim generic anti -inflammatory effects of their 
products. Does the FDA consider reduction of inflammation as one of the main functions of native SVF or resident MSCs (from bone marrow, adipose tissue etc.)? I f not, it should be clearly 
stated that use of purified SVF or unexpanded MSCs for most conditions, including lung diseases, is non- homologous use and their putative anti -inflammatory effects arise from more 
than minimal manipulation.

3) “Human Cells, Ti ssues, and Cellular and Tissue -Based Products (HCT/Ps) from 
Adipose Tissue: Regulatory Considerations” 
The fact that the FDA has developed a draft guidance specifically for HCT/Ps derived from 
adipose tissue indicates that the Agency is determined to take a more active stance against the unregulated use of SVF as cell/tissue therapy. We welcome this development since we have witnessed the proliferation of outfits offering SVF as treatment for several respiratory diseases with no proof of long term safety or  of efficacy. Therefore, we agree with the FDA that

inform future FDA actions regarding outfits treating patient with SVF. It is critical to make this binding and/or have clear methods of enforcement.

Answers Regarding the Scope of the Exception”  
The purpose of this guidance is to further clarify the so- called “surgical exemption” (found in 21

this guidance is non- binding, we hope it will lead to better monitoring of “clinics” that offer

This is important since such establishments do not have to register and list with the FDA, essentially operating under the Agency’s radar.

 Please consider the following additional comments, following the public hearing on the Draft

1.  With respect to the issue of whether the concept of “main function” to identify tissue as 
structural or non- structural is new, or is a concept FDA has always applied — 
It is sometimes asserted that FDA has applied the concept of main function to minimal  
manipulation determinations for many years.  This assertion is sometimes supported by a reference to this statement in the May 14, 1998 preamble to the Establishment Registration & 
Listing proposed rule:  “FDA recognizes that some products may have both s ystemic and 
structural effects but intends that a product’s primary effect  be determinative.”  Emphasis added.  
See the Federal Register of May 14, 1998, 63 FR 26744, 26749.  
It must be noted that FDA made the above statement in the context of a discussion of the fourth 
criteria for eligibility for marketing solely under Section 361 of the Public Health Service Act , that 
is, whether or not the HCT/P has a systemic effect.  See 21 CFR 1271.10(a)(4)(i).  This 
statement was not made in the context of a discussi on of minimal manipulation, (21 CFR 
1271.10(a)(1), nor was it made in the context of a discussion of how to determine whether 
tissue should be considered structural or non- structural.  
The cited FDA statement does not support the assertion that FDA has  long applied the concept 
of main function to the identification of tissue as structural or non -structural.   
 
 2.  The Draft Guidance on HCT/Ps from Adipose Tissue states, in Example B -3, page 5:  
The basic function of breast tissue is to produce milk (lactati on) after childbirth.  
Because this is not a basic function of adipose tissue, using HCT/Ps from 
adipose tissues for breast augmentation would generally be considered non-
homologous use.  
Several speakers at the public hearing noted that breast tissue has functions in addition to 
lactation.  
In addition, it should be noted that FDA’s statement misapplies the homologous use regulations.  
According to 21 CFR 1271.3(c), homologous use means the : 
…repair, reconstruction, or supplementation of a recipient’s cell s or tissues with 
an HCT/P that performs the same basic function or functions in the recipient as in the donor.   
Under the regulations, t he correct analysis is to compare the function of the donated tissue in 
the donor, and the function the donated tissue  is expected to perform in the recipient.    2 
 The Draft Guidance on HCT/Ps from Adipose Tissue indicates that in the donor, adipose tissue 
stores energy, insulates the body, and provides cushioning and support for subcutaneous tissues and internal organs.  ( Draft Guidance, page 1.)   
Therefore, in determining whether breast augmentation is a homologous use of adipose tissue, 
the correct question is “Is transplanted adipose tissue expected to store energy, insulate the 
body, or provide cushioning and support f or subcutaneous tissues and internal organs?”   
The correct question is not “Does adipose tissue in the donor have the same basic function as 
breast tissue in the recipient?”   
These two questions may have different answers.  It is important to ask the cor rect question.

Background			For	 the	 backgournd	 and	 key	 definitions,	 please	 see	 the	 TPG’s	 previous	 comments	 to	 the	 docket,	 including	comments	previously	submitted	related	to	minimal	manipulation	and	homologous	use.		As	part	of	its	recent	draft	guidance	document	titled	Homologous	Use	of	Human	Cells,	Tissues,	and	Cellular	and	Tissue-Based	Products:		Draft	Guidance	for	Industry	and	FDA	Staff,	the	FDA	noted	that	“[a]	homologous	use	for	a	cellular	or	nonstructural	tissue	would	generally	be	a	metabolic	or	biochemical	function	in	the	recipient,	such	as,	hematopoietic,	immune,	and	endocrine	functions”.1		In	general,	while	there	may	be	tissue	products	which	present	such	homologous	uses,	for	allograft	HCT/Ps,	it	is	difficult	to	retain	the	361	HCT/P	designation	due	to	the	fourth	key	“kick	up”	factor	(namely,	“[t]he	HCT/P	does	not	have	a	systemic	effect	and	is	not	dependent	upon	the	metabolic	activity	of	living	cells	for	its	primary	function”).		Especially	for	allograft	products	containing	only	cells,	given	the	aforementioned	homologous	uses,	it	is	difficult	to	delinate	cellular	only	tissue	products	which	 would	 not	 be	 dependent	 upon	 the	 metabolic	 activity	 of	 such	 cells.	 	 Due	 to	 this	 limitation,	 this	 draft	guidance	document	recommendation	does	not	directly	address	the	topic.						                                                 1http://www.fda.gov/downloads/BiologicsBloodVaccines/GuidanceComplianceRegulatoryInformation/Guidances/Tissue/UCM469751.pdf    Page 4 Questions	and	Answers	 Biocompatibility	 (1) Should	all	HCT/Ps	undergo	biocompatibility	testing?	Currently,	 the	 FDA	 does	 not	 require	 biocompatibility	 testing	 for	 361	 HCT/Ps;	 the	 FDA	 does	 require	biocompatibility	testing	for	351	HCT/Ps,	consistent	with	ISO	10993.		However,	given	that	ISO	10993	is	for	devices,	 and	 not	 361	 HCT/Ps,	 there	 are	 some	 limitations	 to	 its	 application,	 including	 a	 broader	 focus	(detailing	 interactions	 with	 metals	 and	 other	 materials	 which	 are	 not	 relevant	 to	 361	 HCT/Ps),	 lack	 of	incorporation	of	innate	variability	in	the	starting	material,	etc.		In	addition,	when	testing	in	animal	models,	some	tests	may	show	a	xenograft	response,	which	may	not	be	a	true	issue	of	biocompatibility.		For	these	reasons	 and	 others,	 not	 all	 of	 the	 biocompatibility	 tests	 outlined	 in	 ISO	 10993	 will	 be	 relevant	 to	 361	HCT/Ps.		A	processer	of	a	361	HCT/P	should	perform	a	risk	analysis,	consistent	with	ISO	10993-1:2009	or	current	version,	 for	 each	 HCT/P	or	 HCT/P	 family,	 to	 ascertain	 what,	 if	 any,	 biocompatibility	 testing	 may	 be	appropriate.		The	risk	analysis	should	focus	on	each	HCT/P	or	HCT/P	family,	given	that	biocompatibility	will	relate	to	each	HCT/P	or	HCT/P	family	due	to	different	processing	steps,	different	material	incorporation	rates	(i.e.	duration	of	contact,	and	in	some	cases,	location	of	implantation),	etc.	In	addition,	a	processer	of	a	HCT/P	should	ensure	that	material	intended	for	use	as	containers	or	packaging	for	HCT/Ps	be	compliant	with	USP	(U.S.	Pharmacopoeia)	Class	VI	testing	requirements.			Beyond	the	risk	analysis,	a	processer	may	also	want	to	perform	biocompatibility	tests	(as	further	outlined	in	ISO	10993),	as	deemed	appropriate	by	the	risk	analysis.		Any	biocompatibility	tests	should	target	the	highest	risk	areas	highlighted	in	the	risk	analysis.				The	risk	analysis	will	highlight	which	of	the	available	biocompatibility	tests	may	be	most	relevant	for	the	HCT/P	or	HCT/P	family.		To	determine	which	biocompatibility	tests	would	be	most	relevant,	a	processer	may	want	to	examine	issues	related	to	material	duration	of	contact,	frequency	of	application,	and	location	of	implantation.	Knowing	the	processing	components	that	could	or	are	encountered	by	the	HCT/P	and	any	existing	information	(publically	available	or	internally	developed)	that	supports	history	of	safe	use	of	these	components	can	also	aid	in	determining	necessary	tests.	Any	local	and/or	systemic	effects	of	the	tissue	form	in	its	final	configuration	should	be	considered.		Whenever	possible,	testing	should	be	performed	to	demonstrate	 the	 safety	 of	 any	 residual	 processing	 chemicals	 or	 materials.	 		A	 justification	 as	 to	 why	 no	further	 testing	 or	 no	 testing	 is	 required	 should	 be	 documented.		For	 the	 format	 and	 content	 of	 the	analysis,	a	tissue	processer	may	want	to	refer	to	FDA’s	guidance	document	on	this	topic	(and,	in	particular,	Annex	C	of	“Use	of	International	Standard	ISO	10993-1,	Biological	evaluation	of	medical	devices	-	Part	1:	Evaluation	and	testing	within	a	risk	management	process”).		(2) When	should	a	processer	perform	an	initial,	or	update	an	existing,	risk	analysis?	An	initial	or	updated	risk	analysis	should	be	performed	when	implementing	a	new	tissue	form,	instituting	a	change	to	an	existing	tissue	process,	sterilization	method	(if	applicable),	instituting	a	change	in	the	physical	configuration	 (e.g.,	 size,	 geometry,	 surface	 properties),	 changing	the	 manufacturer’s	 objective	 intent. Appropriate	Analysis,	Characterization	and	Assessment		(5) What	are	some	examples	of	the	original,	relevant	characteristics	of	structural	tissue?	Examples	 of	the	 original,	relevant	 characteristics	 of	 structural	tissues	 include	 strength,	 flexibility,	cushioning,	covering,	compressibility,	and	response	to	friction	and	shear.		(6) What	about	endogenous	cells	in	structural	tissues?	Structural	 tissues	 may	 contain	 endogenous	cells.	 These	 endogenous	 cells,	 whether	 viable	 or	non-viable,	may	serve	to	maintain	the	basic	function	of	the	structural	tissue.	Examples	of	these	include	cartilage,	bone,	skin	for	burns	and	amnionic	membrane	that	contain	viable	or	non-viable	cells.			(7) How	does	the	homologous	use	relate	to	minimal	manipulation?	As	 noted	 in	 the	 flow	 diagram	 below	 (Diagram	 A),	 the	 manufacturer’s	 objective	 intent	 (including	 the	associated	 claims)	 delineate	the	 particular	 homologous	 uses	 of	 the	 HCT/P.	 	 Those	 homologous	 uses	describe	the	basic	function	or	functions	relevant	to	such	HCT/P.		To	ascertain	whether	a	HCT/P	is	minimally	manipulated,	 the	 processer	 must	 ensure	 that	 the	 processing	 steps	 do	 not	 alter	 the	original,	 relevant	characterics	 that	 support	 the	 basic	 function	 or	 functions	 of	 such	 HCT/P.	 	 	 To	 ascertain	 whether	 those	original,	 relevant	 characteristics	 have	 been	 retained,	specific	 analysis,	 characterization,	 or	 assessment	should	 occur	 and	 the	 results	 must	 be	 within	 appropriate	 parameters	 defined	 for	 success. Dear FDA,<br/>I&#39;m writing to comment in support of your 4 recent draft guidances pertaining to HCT/Ps and in particular as they relate to investigational cell and specifically stem cell therapies. I urge you to finalize these draft guidances as soon as possible and importantly to begin taking regulatory action based upon them. <br/><br/>To me as a stem cell biologist interested in clinical translation and as someone who has been following the stem cell treatment field for a long time, my view is that the 4 draft guidances are positive steps that make good sense both from a common sense and scientific perspective. <br/><br/>More than minimally manipulated cellular products and those used in non-homologous fashion (these are common occurrences amongst the hundreds of stem cell clinics in the US today) pose greater risks to patients. More specifically, I don&#39;t see how adipose stem cells (aka fat stem cells, SVF) can be commonly used in a homologous fashion except in rare instances such as in tissues with a sizable normal adipose constituency. I also agree with the example in the draft guidance of SVF as a more than minimally manipulated product. There is in addition common, risky non-homologous use of bone marrow, blood or placental/amniotic cells amongst hundreds of American clinics from coast to coast. <br/><br/>As the US stem cell clinic industry continues to grow (I would predict there are more than 600 clinics selling non-FDA approved stem cell interventions today) there is an ever-growing need for clear, consistent, and proportionate regulatory oversight activity. To date we haven&#39;t seen that. <br/><br/>An industry this size largely operating without FDA approval or licensing of products via INDs and BLAs, is putting patients at great risk and it also jeopardizes the FDA&#39;s overall authority if the agency only takes minimal action (e.g. 1 or less warning letters per year from CBER). Finally, this situation poses serious risks to the exciting and promising legitimate stem cell and regenerative medicine field.<br/><br/>While advocates for clinics including some very sincere patients often invoke the importance of making &#39;their own&#39; stem cells available to them and these can be very emotional and moving kinds of statements, the status quo of hundreds of clinics selling a hodge-podge of investigational stem cells to patients outside of the traditional clinical trial process is not benefiting the patients in any proven way and is instead putting them at sizable risk of harm.<br/><br/>For instance, in recent weeks we have heard about serious adverse events in patients who received unapproved stem cell interventions from clinics including a number of blinded patients and other adverse events have also occurred but are not yet in the public domain, making it all the more urgent that you approve the draft guidances and begin a period of proportionate regulatory activity related to stem cell clinics.  <br/><br/>Thank you and best regards,<br/>Paul Knoepfler<br/>Professor<br/>Cell Biology &amp; Human Anatomy<br/>UC Davis School of Medicine
